# The Stillwind Spire: design

Working title. The name lives in one place, `title` in
`story/0-game.story`; the program is named after this directory
(`build.sh`), so renaming the game is one line and renaming the program
is one directory, its C file and its two test files.

## Premise

In the valley of Hallow Mere the wind stopped one afternoon in spring,
and the evening stopped with it. The sun has sat in the same notch of the
mountains ever since, a long gold hour that nobody can get to the end of.
The mill does not turn, the washing does not dry, the dandelion clocks
stand in the meadow with every seed still on them, and the boats lie on
the mere like pictures of boats. People were charmed for about a week.

**Ysolde Fenn** surveyed weather for the county for forty years and has
been retired from it for six. She knows that winds do not stop on their
own; they are kept, and in this valley they are kept in the **Stillwind
Spire**, the old tower at the head of the meadow, where each floor holds
one bound wind. The keeper, **Wenna Hale**, has not been seen since the
afternoon it all went quiet. So Ysolde takes her walking stick (the brass
vane on its top has pointed the same way since spring, which offends her
professionally) and walks up to see.

What she finds, floor by floor, is not a curse but a series of small
wrongs: a cork where a cork should not be, a nest in a rain-spout, a
music box with no key. Behind them all is **Tamsin**, Wenna's
eleven-year-old granddaughter, who was left to mind the tower "until
evening" while her grandmother sailed down the mere to fetch a new
rain-bringer. Tamsin stopped the winds so that it would stay evening
until Gran came home, which is reasonable; but with no wind, Gran's boat
cannot sail home, which is the snag. The game ends when Tamsin, shown
the becalmed sail through the parapet's spyglass, lets the last wind go
herself, and the sun at last goes down.

The tone is gentle and dry: nobody dies, nothing is evil, the jokes are
Ysolde's and the tower's. Puzzles are fair and generous: every object
that solves something is described as being for something, nothing can
be lost for good (things you cannot take back are refused, kindly), the
game says so when a door will stay shut for a while, and UNDO, HINT,
SAVE and RESTORE are always there.

## The player's goal

Free the winds of the Spire, find out who bound them and why, and let the
evening end. Score: points for each wind freed, each of the smaller
puzzles, and the ending.

## Characters

- **Ysolde Fenn** (the player). Sixty-odd, long blue-green coat, grey
  hair in a bun, sensible boots, a satchel, and the vane-staff: a walking
  stick with a small brass weathervane on top, which turns again, a
  little more, with each wind she frees. Practical, curious, quietly
  funny; talks to objects.
- **Clementine**, the keeper's goat. Wears the tower's door key on a red
  ribbon because "the safest place for a key is on a goat". Polite in her
  way. Will trade.
- **Tamsin Hale**, eleven, the apprentice. A voice in the speaking-tube
  for most of the game ("Nobody's in! Come back after evening!"), a
  scamper of footsteps on the stair, then, at the top, herself: stubborn,
  clever, homesick for her grandmother.
- **Wenna Hale**, the keeper. Off stage: her notes pinned up around the
  tower, her sail far down the mere.
- **Hob Tarrant**, the miller, asleep on his own millstone because there
  is nothing else to do with it.
- **Mister Lope**, the brass hare on the Spire's roof that serves it for
  a weathercock. Vain, very polished, stuck facing east since spring and
  sick of the view.
- **The winds**, each a character in its vessel: **Old Parch**, the dry
  east wind, a cantankerous wheeze in the great bellows; **Mother
  Mizzle**, the rain-bringer, sighing in a glass carboy; **Hush**, the
  night breeze, too shy to come out of a music box. The fourth, the
  **Homeward** (west) wind, is in Tamsin's paper pinwheel.

## Scenes

Ten painted scenes; the first three are built (marked *). Each has a
walkable floor, depth (the character smaller toward the back), exits at
its edges and doors, hotspots for its things, and scenery that hides the
character when she walks behind it.

| 1* | **The Meadow Path** | the head of the valley | the Spire up the path, the scratched boulder, dandelion clocks that will not blow, Clementine grazing with the key on her ribbon; tracks west to the mill and east to the mere |
| 2* | **The Spire's Foot** | outside the door | the great arched door (locked), Wenna's note, an apple tree with one stubborn apple, a rain barrel (dry), the boot-scraper |
| 3* | **The Hall of Vanes** | the ground floor | a round hall, a cold hearth with a pair of bellows (split), a rack of old weathervanes, the speaking-tube, and the stair behind the wind-gate, a turnstile of little sails that turns only with a draught |
| 4 | **The Still Mill** | west of the meadow | Hob asleep on the millstone, the sails stopped, the corkscrew on his belt |

| 7 | **The Rain Loft** | second floor | Mother Mizzle in her carboy, the spout outside blocked by a nest |

## Puzzles

1. **The Goat's Key** (scenes 1, 2). The door is locked; Wenna's note
   says the key is "with Clementine", and the key is on Clementine's
   ribbon. Clementine will not be parted from it for nothing. At the
   Spire's foot the apple tree has one apple left, out of reach: in any
   ordinary year the wind would have brought it down in autumn. KNOCK
   APPLE WITH STAFF (or SHAKE TREE: Ysolde's arms are not up to it, the
   staff is) brings it down. GIVE APPLE TO GOAT: Clementine trades the
   key, ribbon and all. UNLOCK DOOR WITH KEY, OPEN DOOR, IN. *Built.*
2. **The Wind-Gate** (scenes 2, 3). The stair is behind a turnstile of
   little sails that only a draught will turn ("THE WIND LETS YOU UP").
   The bellows by the hearth would make one, but their leather is split
   along a seam. TIE RIBBON TO BELLOWS (the ribbon from Clementine's key)
   mends them; BLOW GATE WITH BELLOWS (or PUMP BELLOWS at the gate) turns
   the sails and opens the stair. *Built; the stair leads to floors not
   yet built, and says so.*
3. **Old Parch** (scenes 4, 6). The east wind is corked into the great
   bellows. The cork is in too deep for fingers; Hob the miller has a
   corkscrew, and would give it to anyone who could get his mill going,
   which nobody can, so Ysolde borrows it while he sleeps and promises
   (aloud, to his snoring) to bring it back. Uncorked, Old Parch storms
   out of the window and the mill's sails turn; Hob wakes, delighted, and
   lets her keep the corkscrew. The dry wind also dries the soaked stair
   rope to the loft (until then it slips through the hands).
4. **Mother Mizzle** (scene 7, then 2). The rain-bringer cannot rain
   because the loft's spout is blocked by a wren's nest, eggs and all.
   Ysolde must not break it: she lifts it out in her satchel, carries it
   down and sets it in the apple tree. The rain comes; the rain barrel at
   the Spire's foot fills, and its wooden float rises with the music-box
   key that Tamsin hid at the bottom, tied to it.
5. **Hush** (scene 8). The night breeze lives in a music box and comes
   out only to its tune; the box has no key. With the key from the
   barrel, WIND MUSIC BOX: the tune plays, Hush slips out, and the sun
   finally moves: dusk, then the first stars, by which the star chart
   shows the way up (the hatch to the Lamp Room is painted as a star and
   opens only in starlight).
6. **The Homeward Wind** (scenes 9, 10, 5). Tamsin will not let go of
   her pinwheel: if the last wind goes, the evening ends, and Gran said
   she'd be back by evening. On the parapet Mister Lope, flattered,
   admits he can see something "very white and very still" down the
   mere; the spyglass shows Wenna's becalmed sail. SHOW SPYGLASS TO
   TAMSIN (or ask her to look): she understands, and lets the Homeward
   wind out of the pinwheel herself. Ending at the Mere Shore: the sail
   comes home in the dusk.

No dead ends: the apple, the nest and the ribbon cannot be lost (eating
the apple, dropping the nest, giving the ribbon away are refused with a
reason); the goat gives the key back for nothing once the door is open;
the corkscrew can always be borrowed again.

## Tone and look

Flat-colour vector painting under a long gold evening: deep blue sky
going to apricot at the horizon, lilac-blue mountains, greens warm in the
low light, the tower's stone cream on its sunny side and violet-grey in
shadow, light always from the left (the sun in its notch). Interiors are
lamplit amber against cool stone. One palette for every scene, so the
character (blue-green coat, cream scarf, brass vane) reads in all of
them. Outlines are painted with `stroke` where they carry the shape, so
the phosphor style (where nothing is filled) still reads as a line
drawing.
