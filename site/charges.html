Charges and limits

This page collects in one place what every call costs and every fixed number of the system: the machine, each device, the content device and the network. Each value is explained in the article that owns it; here they are only listed. Values marked provisional are placeholders that may change once weak hosts have been measured (SPEC §provisional lists the core's, and each device document in spec/ its own); every other value is fixed for version 0.

The budget, in one line

Each frame adds 125,000 units to the budget and the CPU runs while the budget is above zero; an overshoot carries into the next frame, unused budget never does, and wait_frame throws away the rest of the frame.

A device call is **deferred** when its result is one the program could work out without the device (a constant, an argument, or a simple function of the arguments) and its charge depends only on its arguments; it is **answered** when its result or charge depends on the device's state. A host that keeps a device in another process can send deferred calls in batches without waiting for them; an answered call needs a round trip, which the 64 pays for. The calls of the system device and Machines are the machine's own and cost 4 whatever they return. The call table of the article The machine gives every call's class.

Content delivered at completion is what a get, load, find or records-style request (subscriptions, timeline, names, programs, records) writes to its buffer. Nothing else is charged: a rejected submit copies nothing, the fields a completion writes into a request block are free, and laying out the page is free (the host does it outside the frames).

Two examples. A deferred call that copies nothing costs 5 units from ROM (the ecall and 4), an answered one 65. Writing a whole 80-column row costs 165 with one write of 80 bytes, 85 with one cells_write of 80 cells, against at least 560 for 80 one-byte writes.

What a frame asks of the host

The budget bounds the program's own work. Drawing, hiding lines and laying out text are the host's, and are bounded by the limits below instead: the terminal's 2,400 cells, the two vector displays' ink caps (a list over its cap is cut where it passes it, the rest not drawn), the 3D display's other caps, and the page's layout outside the frames. They are set so that every device at its cap at once costs the reference host no more than one frame's time on one thread; no legal program can make a frame slow.

In a system the machines the shell shows also share one budget, the system ink: 691,200 square units of the shell's screen, what one machine shown over the whole screen may count. The pictures count their areas first, then each machine's lists their ink times the picture's scale; a picture that does not fit is a plain plate, a list is cut where the ink runs out. The shell's own screen is not counted. So a whole system asks of the host about what two machines can (Screens, panels and input gives the rules).

Performance

Tersenet's speed is specified in three tiers, collected in PERFORMANCE.md and checked by terse perf:

  • **Requirements**, in units any implementation can be tested against on any machine: frames, round trips, and what may not wait on what. A held article is shown with no request to any relay; a blob not held costs one round trip; a content request that needs no network completes within 2 frames; a program the shell starts runs within 2 frames; a host's first frame waits on nothing but the image, its settings and the fonts they list. They pass or fail.
  • **Ceilings**, in milliseconds or frames, far above what a correct implementation takes on any reasonable computer (a 2-core 2 GHz machine of the last ten years): a host's first frame within 1.5 s, a held article shown by a whole process within 250 ms, a worst legal frame drawn on one thread within 4 frames. They fail on any machine: going over one means something is wrong, not that the computer is slow.
  • **Targets**, the reference implementation's times on a named machine: a first frame within 50 ms, a held article within 25 ms, a worst legal frame within one frame. They are information, and fail only with terse perf --strict.

Ceilings and targets judge the median of several runs, so one slow run on a busy machine fails nothing. An implementation is expected to run terse perf (another host or reader plugs in with --host or --reader) and pass every requirement and ceiling.
